# Causal inference reveals mechanism-driven freshwater fish distribution under climate change: a river network perspective

## Abstract

Freshwater biodiversity faces accelerating threats from climate change, yet prediction frameworks remain largely correlative and fail to distinguish causal mechanisms from spurious associations. Here we integrate causal discovery with species distribution modeling (SDM) at the river network scale across China, using 1-km resolution stream-specific environmental layers. For a representative freshwater fish species (*Carassius auratus*), we combined four SDM algorithms (Maxent, Random Forest, GAM, Neural Network) with constraint-based and score-based causal structure learning to map stable environmental dependencies. The models achieved robust discrimination (AUC 0.81–0.91) on independent test data. Causal network analysis identified stable directed relationships among hydrological accumulation, seasonal hydroclimatic variability, and topographic gradients, distinguishing them from mere correlations. Conditional average treatment effects (CATE) estimated via causal forests revealed spatially heterogeneous intervention windows where habitat suitability is most sensitive to environmental change. To address extrapolation risks, we retrained models using only "scenario-available" variables for future projections (2041–2060) under four SSP pathways. Projected changes indicate a consistent decline in habitat suitability, with structural uncertainty (cross-model variance) exceeding scenario uncertainty. Our mechanism-consistent framework enables prioritization of climate-sensitive river segments for adaptive monitoring and conservation, advancing from correlation-based to causally informed freshwater biodiversity forecasting.

**Keywords:** Causal discovery, Species distribution model, River network, Freshwater fish, Climate change, Conditional treatment effect

## 1. Introduction

Freshwater ecosystems harbor disproportionate biodiversity on less than 1% of Earth's surface, sustaining over 10% of all described species and roughly one-third of vertebrate diversity (Dudgeon et al., 2006; WWF, 2020). Yet population declines and range contractions in rivers and lakes are progressing faster than in terrestrial or marine systems, driven by the combined effects of flow regulation, pollution, invasive species, overexploitation, and rapid climate warming (Olden et al., 2010; He and Silliman, 2019; Reid et al., 2019). China hosts some of the world's most diverse and heavily modified river basins, where climate change interacts with land-use intensification and water infrastructure to reshape habitat conditions for freshwater fishes. Understanding how these multiple pressures propagate through river networks to affect species distributions is therefore both an ecological challenge and an urgent conservation priority.

Species distribution models (SDMs) have become the dominant quantitative framework for mapping habitat suitability, forecasting climate-driven range shifts, and informing conservation planning (Guisan and Zimmermann, 2000; Elith and Leathwick, 2009; Guisan et al., 2013; Peterson et al., 2011). Correlative SDMs, including Maxent, Random Forests, and Generalized Additive Models, can capture complex nonlinear associations between species occurrence and environmental predictors and are now routinely assembled into ensembles to increase robustness (Araújo and New, 2007; Thuiller et al., 2009; Hao et al., 2020; Valavi et al., 2021). However, these approaches typically operate in a purely associational paradigm. They excel at identifying spatial patterns of co-occurrence but provide limited mechanistic insight into which environmental changes directly govern distributional shifts, and they often transfer poorly to novel environmental conditions such as future climates (Pearson and Dawson, 2003; Yates et al., 2018).

This limitation is particularly acute in riverine ecosystems, where directional connectivity, upstream–downstream accumulation, and catchment integration create strong spatial dependencies that standard gridded climate products do not represent (Poff et al., 1997; Domisch et al., 2015; Linke et al., 2019). For aquatic organisms confined to dendritic river networks, exposure is defined not only by local temperature or precipitation but also by the cumulative legacy of upstream land use, flow alteration, and geomorphology. Yet most freshwater SDM applications still approximate exposure by overlaying river lines on coarse terrestrial climate grids, obscuring key causal pathways such as temperature modulation by elevation gradients or nutrient enrichment by aggregated agricultural runoff. At the same time, uncertainty in projections is dominated by structural differences among algorithms and variable sets, but is rarely decomposed explicitly relative to scenario uncertainty (Lawler et al., 2006; Thuiller, 2004; Thuiller et al., 2019).

Recent advances in causal discovery and machine-learning–based effect estimation offer a path to move beyond correlation toward mechanism-aware biodiversity forecasts (Pearl, 2000; Peters et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2020; Su et al., 2021). Graphical causal models and structure-learning algorithms can identify stable dependency patterns among climate, hydrology, land use, and topography (Kalisch and Bühlmann, 2007; Spirtes et al., 2000), while Double Machine Learning and Causal Forests enable estimation of average and heterogeneous treatment effects in high-dimensional settings (Chernozhukov et al., 2018; Athey and Imbens, 2016; Wager and Athey, 2018). In this study, we integrate these causal tools with a multi-algorithm SDM ensemble to develop an integrated causal–predictive framework for freshwater fish distributions across China's river networks, using *Carassius auratus* as a representative species. Specifically, we construct river-network–consistent environmental exposure layers at 1-km resolution, learn consensus causal networks linking hydrology, climate, topography, and land cover, estimate both average and spatially heterogeneous treatment effects of key drivers on occurrence, and decompose structural versus scenario uncertainty in mid-century climate projections. Together, these components provide a mechanism-consistent basis for prioritizing climate-sensitive river segments for adaptive monitoring and conservation.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Study Area and Species Occurrence Data

We delineated our study area to encompass the entire mainland China river network system (73.95°E–134.45°E, 18.25°N–53.34°N). This geographic extent captures extraordinary environmental diversity, ranging from the subtropical monsoon regions in the southeast to the arid continental climates in the northwest and the alpine environments of the Tibetan Plateau. The domain includes major river basins such as the Yangtze, Yellow, Pearl, and Songhua rivers, which together span strong gradients in temperature, precipitation, and human footprint. We defined river network pixels using a flow accumulation threshold of ≥100 cells (approximately 100 km² upstream contributing area), yielding approximately 2.1 million river pixels at a 1-km spatial resolution. Representing the full river network at this resolution allows us to capture both large mainstem channels and smaller tributaries that may act as climate refugia or dispersal corridors for freshwater fishes. All spatial data were projected to the Albers Conic Equal Area coordinate system to ensure accurate area calculations.

Species occurrence records for *Carassius auratus* were compiled from three complementary sources to maximize spatial coverage: (i) peer-reviewed literature extracted from systematic reviews; (ii) FishBase; and (iii) the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF). We implemented a rigorous quality control pipeline including coordinate validation, spatial filtering to national boundaries, precision checks, and deduplication. To reduce sampling bias, common in opportunistic datasets, we applied spatial thinning, retaining only one record per 0.09° (approximately 10 km) grid cell. The final dataset contained n=517 spatially independent occurrence records. The spatial distribution of these occurrence records within the river network is shown in **Figure 1**. *Carassius auratus* is a small-bodied, generalist cyprinid that occupies lentic and low-velocity lotic habitats, including reservoirs, ponds, and lowland floodplains, and is known for its tolerance to eutrophication and moderate hypoxia. These traits, together with its close association with human-modified waters, make it a tractable model species for studying how climate- and land-use–driven environmental changes propagate through river networks. To approximate the available environmental space, we generated background points representing "available" aquatic habitat using Poisson-disk sampling restricted to the river network mask. We enforced a minimum inter-point distance of 5 km and a 5:1 background-to-presence ratio, yielding 1680 background points and ensuring a balanced representation of the available environment while limiting clustering in heavily sampled regions (Wisz et al., 2008; Comte and Olden, 2021).

### 2.2 Environmental Variable Assembly

We assembled environmental predictors from four distinct domains, explicitly accounting for river network topology and upstream–downstream connectivity. First, we calculated hydrological network topology metrics, including flow accumulation, flow path length, and stream order, to quantify catchment-scale connectivity and position within the network. Second, using the EarthEnv-Streams dataset, we extracted monthly mean temperature and precipitation, weighted by upstream catchment area, alongside quarterly seasonality metrics. This upstream weighting ensures that the environmental values reflect the integrated conditions of the entire catchment draining to a point, rather than just the local conditions.

Third, we derived topographic gradients from SRTM digital elevation models, aggregating metrics such as elevation, slope, and relief to the 1-km stream pixel resolution. Fourth, we calculated upstream-weighted land cover and soil fractions using the Consensus Land Cover dataset and SoilGrids250m. This provided variables representing the proportion of varying land use types (e.g., forest, urban, agriculture) and soil properties (e.g., clay content, pH) within the upstream catchment. Hydrological and climatic variables were chosen to represent long-term mean conditions and intra-annual variability that are expected to influence metabolism, reproduction, and overwinter survival in temperate freshwater fishes, while land-cover and soil attributes capture human modification and substrate properties that can alter habitat quality and nutrient regimes (Filipe et al., 2013; Olden et al., 2010).

From an initial set of over 100 candidates, we reduced multicollinearity via zero-variance removal, pairwise correlation screening (|r| > 0.8), and iterative Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) removal with a threshold of VIF ≤ 10. This rigorous selection process yielded 47 independent predictors for the final modeling, as summarized in **Table 1**. This selection follows best-practice recommendations for SDM variable selection to avoid unstable parameter estimates and inflated predictor importance (Dormann et al., 2013; Guisan and Zimmermann, 2000).

### 2.3 Species Distribution Modeling (SDM)

We employed a multi-algorithm ensemble approach to capture the complex non-linear relationships between the species and its environment. The combined dataset (n=2016) was partitioned into stratified training (80%) and test (20%) sets, preserving the prevalence of presences and background across major environmental gradients. We utilized four distinct algorithms that span a continuum from parametric to machine-learning models:
1.  **Maximum Entropy (Maxent)**: Modeled using the `maxnet` R package with flexible feature transformations (linear, quadratic, product, threshold, and hinge) to approximate the species' potential geographic distribution (Phillips et al., 2006; Elith et al., 2011; Merow et al., 2013).
2.  **Random Forest (RF)**: An ensemble of 500 regression trees, selected for its ability to handle high-dimensional interactions, nonlinear responses, and collinearity among predictors while remaining relatively robust to overfitting (Breiman, 2001).
3.  **Generalized Additive Model (GAM)**: Fitted with penalized thin-plate regression splines and spatial tensor product smooths to model non-linear responses while maintaining partial interpretability of smooth effects (Hastie and Tibshirani, 1986; Elith and Leathwick, 2009).
4.  **Neural Network (NN)**: A single-hidden-layer feed-forward network with weight decay, capable of learning complex patterns and interactions that may be difficult to capture with more constrained models.

Together, these algorithms provide complementary perspectives on the species–environment relationship: GAM emphasizes smooth, interpretable responses, RF and NN capture complex interactions, and Maxent is well suited to presence–background data with flexible feature classes (Elith et al., 2011; Valavi et al., 2021). Rather than selecting a single "best" model, we interpret patterns across algorithms and use their joint behavior to quantify structural uncertainty (Araújo and New, 2007; Thuiller et al., 2009).

Models were evaluated on the independent test set using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), True Skill Statistic (TSS), Sensitivity, Specificity, and the Continuous Boyce Index to ensure robust performance assessment. These metrics summarize discrimination ability across thresholds and prevalence, but high AUC or TSS values do not automatically guarantee transferability or ecological realism, particularly under novel climate conditions (Araújo and Guisan, 2006; Rodríguez-Rey et al., 2019; Yates et al., 2018). We therefore use them in combination with causal analyses and uncertainty partitioning to avoid over-interpreting any single algorithm or metric.

Beyond accuracy, we also derived model-based diagnostics to describe species–environment relationships from a correlative perspective. For each algorithm we computed permutation-based variable importance and partial dependence (response) curves for the 47 predictors. These diagnostics summarize which environmental gradients appear most influential and the overall shape of their marginal associations with occurrence, and they serve as a baseline "xSDM" interpretation that is familiar in the SDM literature. In subsequent sections we explicitly compare these correlative patterns with causal graphs and treatment-effect estimates to separate genuine drivers from proxy variables.

### 2.4 Causal Inference Framework

To move beyond these correlative diagnostics, we integrated causal discovery and effect estimation into our workflow. Causal analyses were conducted on the same standardized 47-variable dataset and later synthesized with xSDM variable importance and response curves to identify a parsimonious set of core environmental impact factors.
1.  **Causal Structure Learning**: We inferred Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) using both the PC algorithm (constraint-based) and the Hill-Climbing algorithm (score-based) to recover stable dependency patterns among climate, hydrology, topography, land cover, and species occurrence (Kalisch and Bühlmann, 2007; Spirtes et al., 2000; Peters et al., 2017). For each bootstrap sample of the data, we estimated a DAG and then summarized edge stability across 300 replicates, retaining only those edges with a stability frequency ≥ 0.55 to construct a consensus causal network. This stability-based filtering reduces the influence of sampling noise and weak conditional independencies that may arise in high-dimensional settings.
2.  **Average Treatment Effect (ATE)**: We used Double Machine Learning (DML) to estimate the causal effect of key variables on species presence. DML allows for approximately unbiased estimation of causal parameters in the presence of high-dimensional nuisance parameters (confounders) by using separate machine-learning models to predict both the outcome and the treatment, followed by orthogonalization that removes first-order estimation error (Chernozhukov et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2020). We focused on interpretable treatment variables such as urban land-cover fraction, precipitation, and upstream soil properties that correspond to plausible management levers.
3.  **Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE)**: We estimated spatially heterogeneous treatment effects using Causal Forests, which adapt random forests to estimate heterogeneity in causal effects across the covariate space (Athey and Imbens, 2016; Wager and Athey, 2018; Su et al., 2021). For each treatment variable, the CATE surface reveals where marginal changes in that variable would yield the largest positive or negative changes in occurrence probability, providing a basis for spatially targeted interventions.

### 2.5 Future Climate Projections and Uncertainty Decomposition

We projected species distributions to the mid-century period (2041–2060) under four CMIP6 Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. To avoid extrapolation risks associated with variables for which future projections are unavailable or highly uncertain (e.g., detailed land use or soil properties), we reconfigured the SDM ensemble into two families of scenario-consistent models. First, we retrained all four algorithms on a "19+4" predictor set comprising 19 bioclimatic variables and four static layers (elevation, slope, geology, and soil properties). Current bioclimatic conditions were taken from river-network–consistent EarthEnv-Streams layers and renamed to match the WorldClim bioclim schema (bio01–bio19), while future conditions were provided by CMIP6 WorldClim projections from the BCC-CSM2-MR model for 2041–2060 under each SSP. Second, we constructed a parsimonious causal-subset model family by intersecting the core drivers identified in the causal analysis (Section 3.2) with the 19+4 feature space via a one-to-one mapping between hydrological bioclim indices (hydro_wavg_k) and WorldClim bioclim dimensions (bio0k), retaining only those bioclim variables together with elevation and slope. Both model families therefore operate in a common, scenario-available feature space that is explicitly linked to the causal graph, ensuring that projections remain within the domain of the training data while allowing a direct comparison between full and simplified causal SDMs.

For each SSP and algorithm, we generated continuous suitability maps for both the full 19+4 ensemble and the causal-subset models and summarized ensemble behavior across two orthogonal dimensions of uncertainty. Structural Uncertainty was defined as the standard deviation of predictions across the four modeling algorithms for a given scenario, reflecting differences in algorithmic assumptions and flexibility (Lawler et al., 2006; Thuiller et al., 2019). Scenario Uncertainty was defined as the standard deviation of predictions across the four SSP pathways for a given algorithm, reflecting divergence among emission and climate trajectories. We used variance partitioning to compare the relative magnitudes of these uncertainty sources across the study area and to identify river segments where conservation recommendations are robust to both structural and scenario uncertainty.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Model Performance and Overall Fit

The four core algorithms demonstrated robust predictive performance on the independent test set. `Maxnet` achieved the highest discrimination capability with an AUC of 0.909 and a sensitivity of 0.896, indicating a strong ability to correctly identify presence locations. `Random Forest` (RF) excelled in the True Skill Statistic (TSS = 0.699) and specificity (0.789), suggesting it was particularly effective at minimizing false positives. `GAM` provided comparable accuracy (AUC 0.897), offering a balance between complexity and interpretability. The `Neural Network` (NN) maintained acceptable discrimination (AUC 0.813) but was more conservative in predicting background samples compared to the other methods. A detailed comparison of performance metrics across algorithms is presented in **Table 2**. The ensemble of these models provided a comprehensive view of habitat suitability, leveraging the strengths of each algorithmic approach and highlighting areas of model agreement and disagreement.

**Table 2**: Model performance metrics (AUC, TSS, Sensitivity, Specificity, Boyce Index). (See `output/08_model_evaluation/evaluation_summary.csv` for full details).

Overall, discrimination metrics exceeded commonly used thresholds for "good" model performance (AUC > 0.8), indicating that the selected predictors capture key environmental gradients shaping *Carassius auratus* distribution (Elith and Leathwick, 2009; Valavi et al., 2021). However, consistent with previous work on freshwater invasive species and SDM ensembles (Rodríguez-Rey et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2020), we treat these metrics as necessary but not sufficient criteria. High AUC and TSS values can coexist with biased extrapolation or mis-specified causal structures, particularly in heavily human-modified river networks. Our subsequent causal inference and uncertainty decomposition therefore provide an essential second layer of evaluation, clarifying which predictors are likely to be mechanistic drivers and where projections remain fragile.

### 3.2 Key Environmental Drivers and Causal Effects

Multi-model variable-importance summaries and response curves provided an initial correlative view of key environmental gradients. Across algorithms, topographic slope and upstream climatic gradients consistently emerged as influential predictors of *Carassius auratus* occurrence. `Maxnet` and `NN` ranked average elevation (`dem_avg`) and slope variability (`slope_range`) highly, while `RF` emphasized river-network topology metrics such as flow accumulation (`flow_acc`). `GAM` smooth terms and response curves suggested a broad optimum at low to intermediate elevations and gentle to moderate slopes, consistent with the species' association with lowland floodplains and regulated reservoirs. However, these diagnostics inevitably conflate direct and indirect pathways; strongly correlated downstream variables can appear important even when they merely proxy for upstream climatic or land-use drivers.

The inferred causal structure, which disentangles these correlations, is shown in **Figure 2**.

![Figure 2: Consensus Causal DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) showing stable dependencies among variables and species occurrence.](figures/14_causal/dag_hc_avg_network_full.png)

The causal analysis provided a more nuanced view than variable importance rankings. Average Treatment Effect (ATE) estimates based on Double Machine Learning clarified causal contributions, distinguishing them from mere associations (**Table 3**). The proportion of urban built-up area showed a significant positive causal effect on suitability (ATE = 0.247 ± 0.026, p < 10⁻²⁰), suggesting that low-gradient urban water bodies, often enriched with nutrients, remain valuable habitats for this tolerant species. Annual precipitation and upstream clay content were also significantly positive causal drivers. Importantly, positive effects of elevation range and slope variability corroborated the hypothesis that valley habitats within complex terrain provide favorable conditions, likely due to hydrological stability and diverse microhabitats. Together, the overlap between high SDM importance and large ATE values defined a compact set of core drivers that we later used to construct simplified, causally informed SDMs for future projections.

### 3.3 Spatial Suitability Patterns and Uncertainty

Under current climate conditions, high-probability pixels were mainly distributed along the middle and lower reaches of the Yangtze River, the Jianghan Plain, the Pearl River Delta, and the main stream of the Songhua River, as shown in **Figure 3**. These regions correspond to low-elevation, high-flow accumulation areas with substantial water resources. The spatial mean of prediction standard deviation was 0.099, indicating generally high model agreement. However, variance decomposition revealed a striking pattern: structural uncertainty (variance across algorithms) was approximately 4.7 times higher than scenario uncertainty (variance across emission pathways) on average. This suggests that the choice of modeling technique introduces more uncertainty into the projections than the choice of future climate scenario, highlighting the importance of ensemble modeling.

### 3.4 Heterogeneous Response and Local Sensitivity

Local sensitivity analysis showed significant spatial gradients in how the species responds to environmental variables. Southern river segments exhibited the highest average sensitivity to slope variability, implying that small changes in topography in these regions could have outsized effects on suitability. In contrast, central and northern segments showed a stronger negative response to monsoon precipitation variability. CATE analysis (**Figure 4**) revealed that while most river segments showed a small positive response to urban intervention (consistent with the ATE), a distinct negative response tail appeared in the arid northwest. This indicates that in water-scarce regions, urbanization and associated water abstraction may be detrimental, contrasting with the positive effects seen in water-rich regions.

![Figure 4: Spatial maps of Conditional Average Treatment Effects (CATE) for key drivers.](figures/11_prediction_maps/cate_map.png)

### 3.5 Distribution Changes Under Future Scenarios

Projections based on CMIP6 SSP scenarios for the period 2041–2060 showed consistent declining trends in habitat suitability. Compared to the current distribution, `Maxnet` mean suitability in the full 19+4 ensemble decreased by approximately 25%, and `GAM` by about 50%. Spatial prediction maps indicate that current hotspot river segments will shrink significantly, and northern snow-fed rivers will be adversely affected by increased heat and altered hydrological regimes. The direction of decline in high-suitability segments is highly consistent across different SSPs and algorithms, providing a robust signal of climate vulnerability for this species despite the structural uncertainty in magnitude. Causally informed subset models, built from a reduced set of bioclimatic and static predictors, reproduced these broad spatial patterns and magnitudes of decline, indicating that the main conclusions are robust to substantial dimensionality reduction. **Figure 5** visualizes these future trends.

## 4. Discussion


Our integration of causal discovery with species distribution modeling addresses a fundamental limitation of correlative frameworks: the inability to distinguish direct drivers from confounded associations (Pearl, 2000; Peters et al., 2017). In practice, our workflow first uses multi-model variable importance and response curves to provide an intuitive, correlative summary of species–environment relationships, and then overlays causal graphs and treatment-effect estimates to refine this picture. The stable causal network revealed hierarchical dependencies where topography influences climate, which in turn shapes land cover and soil properties to structure habitat suitability. This contrasts with traditional variable importance rankings, which often conflate direct and indirect effects (Elith and Leathwick, 2009; Molnar, 2020). For example, while elevation (`dem_avg`) emerged as highly important across all SDMs, causal analysis demonstrated it acts primarily through mediating seasonal temperature and precipitation rather than as a direct physiological constraint. This mechanistic clarity enables targeted interventions; rather than focusing on "high-elevation conservation," managers can address specific climate–topography interactions, such as maintaining cold-air pooling or managing orographic precipitation inputs. The causal framework allows us to peel back layers of correlation to identify the levers that are actually pulling the strings of biodiversity distribution.

The implementation of river-network consistency transforms the accuracy and interpretability of aquatic SDMs. Most freshwater SDMs approximate exposure using terrestrial grid cells overlaid on river lines, ignoring upstream accumulation and connectivity (Domisch et al., 2015; Karger et al., 2017). By adopting EarthEnv-Streams and related hydro-environmental layers, we captured true aquatic exposure: a river pixel's environment integrates the entire upstream catchment, not just its local 1-km² cell (Barbarossa et al., 2018; Linke et al., 2019). This distinction is critical—`flow_acc` and `flow_length` ranked among the top predictors, reflecting species' sensitivity to catchment-scale disturbances that terrestrial grids miss. Our results generalize to any riverine organism and can incorporate emerging network metrics describing flow regime alteration, connectivity loss, and ecological network structure (Tonkin et al., 2018). They underscore that for riverine species, the "environment" is not a point in space, but a cumulative legacy of the landscape upstream.

Another striking finding is that cross-model variance (structural uncertainty) dwarfed cross-scenario variance, indicating that algorithmic choices dominate prediction uncertainty over emission pathways in this context. This aligns with recent calls to prioritize structural uncertainty quantification but contrasts with the common practice of reporting scenario ranges without model ensembles (Lawler et al., 2006; Thuiller, 2004; Thuiller et al., 2019). Our results suggest that for robust conservation planning, managers should employ multi-model ensembles rather than relying on a single "best" algorithm, and focus on spatial consensus regions while treating divergent zones as requiring adaptive management under deep uncertainty. The "scenario-available variable retraining" strategy proved essential in this regard. By ensuring identical feature spaces between current and future conditions, we avoided extrapolation into unobserved covariate space—a pervasive but often ignored risk for SDMs transferred to future climates (Araújo and New, 2007; Yates et al., 2018).

 The CATE framework further operationalizes causal inference by quantifying *where* environmental changes yield the largest impacts. High-CATE zones in mid-elevation tributaries indicate "leverage points" for restoration investments, whereas negative-CATE lowland urban watersheds may require alternative strategies, such as urban heat island mitigation or stormwater management. This spatial targeting surpasses uniform conservation approaches that ignore effect heterogeneity and resonates with emerging work on spatially explicit climate adaptation planning for freshwater biodiversity (Reid et al., 2019; Tonkin et al., 2018). However, limitations remain. Causal discovery algorithms assume acyclicity and causal sufficiency. While bootstrap stability filtering mitigates spurious edges, residual confounding from unmeasured variables, such as biotic interactions or dispersal barriers, remains possible. Future work should incorporate prior knowledge, integrate joint species distribution models that capture biotic associations (Tikhonov et al., 2020), and test alternative algorithms to further refine these causal graphs.

## 5. Conclusion

In this study we demonstrate that embedding correlative SDMs within a causal inference and river-network framework yields more interpretable and management-relevant projections of freshwater fish distributions under climate change. By combining high-resolution hydro-environmental layers with causal discovery, double machine learning for average treatment effects, and CATE-based local sensitivity analysis, we identify a small set of robust environmental drivers and spatial leverage points that remain consistent across algorithms and emissions scenarios despite substantial structural uncertainty.

Our results highlight that for riverine species, algorithmic choices and the explicit representation of network structure can dominate predictive uncertainty, implying that conservation planning should prioritize multi-model ensembles, scenario-available predictors, and transparent accounting of structural variance. The proposed workflow is directly transferable to other freshwater taxa and regions where comparable data exist, and can be extended with joint species distribution models and dynamic connectivity metrics as these become available. By shifting from a "predict-and-describe" to an "infer-and-intervene" paradigm, our approach provides a concrete template for linking large-scale biodiversity projections to spatially targeted adaptation actions.
